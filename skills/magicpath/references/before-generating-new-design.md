# Before Generating a New Design

**Read this before choosing a visual direction or writing UI for any new MagicPath design.** Use it most actively when the user gives a weak brief: make a coherent, distinctive first pass without asking them to become the art director. MagicPath is a professional design canvas; a working component is the starting point, not the quality bar.

Use these defaults to fill gaps, never to replace explicit user instructions, an attached design system, supplied assets, or an existing design's visual language. If recreating source or extending an established interface, preserve that reference and use the relevant fidelity playbook. Do not apply this creative-direction process to exports, local installation, read-only tasks, or native-shape diagrams.

Follow the live server's setup timing and the skill's canvas-links rule. Reading this guide and posting a brief must not postpone required session startup or visible agent presence. Share the brief before implementation, then continue; this reference adds **no approval gate** beyond the skill's existing gates.

## Turn a vague request into a design decision

1. **Identify the job.** Infer the audience, the main thing they need to understand or do, the important content, and the target form factor from the request and available context. Make a reasonable assumption when these are missing; state only assumptions that materially affect the result. Do not invent product capabilities or business claims.
2. **Decide what success looks like.** A productivity interface needs legibility, useful density, and clear actions. A launch page or portfolio can prioritize atmosphere and a memorable first impression. A consumer product may need both. Do not give every task a giant marketing hero or every brand a dashboard of cards.
3. **Find the constraints before inventing a style.** Read supplied themes, references, selected designs, and assets. Preserve their actual values and character. If the user already chose a palette, font, dark mode, or playful treatment, do not override it with this guide's defaults.
4. **Consider 2–3 genuinely different directions when style is open.** Include an obvious fit and a less-obvious but defensible alternative. Compare audience fit, content, composition, and available assets; choose deliberately. Do not select randomly, reject a good first idea merely because it came first, or substitute novelty for usability.
5. **Commit to one composition and one signature move.** Decide the focal point, supporting hierarchy, primary action, and a distinctive treatment worth executing exceptionally well: an oversized typographic moment, a beautiful image crop, an unusual but useful split, or a precise information layout. Develop one direction fully rather than mixing several unfinished ones.

When the user asks for multiple directions, vary composition, density, typography, image treatment, and interaction emphasis—not just the accent color. Follow the existing frame gate if the screen split is ambiguous.

## Post a short design brief

For an open-ended new design, send a concise chat update using the format below. This is a visible commitment to the result, not a request for permission or a long explanation of your reasoning.

- **Mood candidates:** 2–3 distinct possibilities; omit when the user supplied a direction.
- **Mood chosen:** a specific mood and physical scene, with one sentence connecting it to this product and audience.
- **Palette:** 5–6 hex values with roles—background, surface, primary text, secondary text, accent, and border. Roles may share a value; do not invent six decorative colors. Add semantic colors only as needed.
- **Type:** available or loadable families, real weights, and a compact size scale for display, headings, body, and labels.
- **Direction:** the main layout, primary action, and the one signature treatment that will make this design specific to the brief.

If the user already supplied a detailed system, briefly confirm what you will preserve and the composition you will build; do not propose competing moods or repeat their entire spec. If they asked for output only, keep the brief internal. Do not wait for a reply unless an existing gate applies.

## Choose a visual direction

Choose a mood before choosing hex values. Anchor it in a material, place, light condition, or visual medium; name the objects that supply the colors. A scene such as fog over a working harbor is more actionable than "modern, premium, clean." Derive the type, imagery, and composition from that same idea so the palette does not feel pasted onto an unrelated template.

For personality-led consumer or marketing work, consider an expressive or playful direction instead of automatically defaulting to corporate restraint. Choose one or two devices that suit the product—an accent duo, an offset shadow, an expressive wordmark, a sticker-like label, or witty copy. Reduce other decoration to compensate. Do not force playfulness on a serious workflow or turn every playful brief into confetti and mascots.

## Build the composition before decorating it

- **Spend detail where attention belongs.** Establish one dominant element per view; let secondary information support it. Use scale, placement, and space before reaching for effects. A dashboard's working area can be the focal point; it does not need a promotional headline.
- **Prefer fewer, better-resolved elements.** Remove redundant badges, empty sections, ornamental dividers, and filler metrics. Restraint means removing noise, not hiding information or controls needed for the task.
- **Group by relationship.** Use tighter spacing within a group and a visibly larger interval between groups. Choose a small spacing scale, then vary it deliberately; uniform gaps flatten hierarchy.
- **Let content live on surfaces.** Use alignment, headings, whitespace, and rules before boxing everything into cards. Containers should express containment, interaction, or comparison—not fill space. Use tables and lists when people need to scan or compare.
- **Use asymmetry when it helps.** A strong grid supports asymmetry; it does not require every column to look identical. Keep operational controls predictable and accessible. Do not introduce arbitrary offsets to simulate art direction.
- **Add a human detail, not a decoration quota.** A thoughtful label, distinctive photograph, warm material, or considered empty state can make a restrained interface inviting.

Apply the skill's Design Defaults throughout: fluid sizing, appropriate frame dimensions, no unrequested device chrome, and complete interactions. Decide how the composition reflows before implementing it, rather than shrinking a desktop layout to fit a phone.

## Make typography and color do the work

### Typography

- Treat type as structure. Choose a readable UI/body face and, when useful, one complementary display face. Use at most two families unless the supplied system calls for more. If a font cannot be loaded, choose a deliberate fallback; never rely on an unavailable family name to achieve the design.
- Use 3–5 clear type levels with meaningful differences in size, weight, and line height. Large display type can use tighter tracking; body copy and small labels need room. Avoid making every heading heavy or every label uppercase.
- Use editorial scale contrast for expressive work, but prioritize scanning and useful density in tools. Generally start body text around 14–16px in product UI and 16–18px for reading-heavy or marketing content; adjust to the actual font and context. Do not make 12px-or-smaller text the default aesthetic.
- Control line length and wrapping. Give headlines intentional breaks that remain sensible when resized; do not depend on manual breaks that collapse at another width. Use tabular numerals for comparable values and consistent baselines for labels.

### Color and surfaces

- Default to a light scheme when no context suggests otherwise. Pure white is a valid everyday ground, especially for typographic work or saturated accents. Cream and off-white are specific aesthetic choices, not an automatic way to make any design feel refined. Honor dark-mode requests and context that genuinely benefits from darkness.
- Give each color a job. Use one primary accent to anchor actions or attention; repeat it consistently. Reserve a strong color moment for what matters instead of saturating every surface. Accent color is not automatically suitable for small text on the background.
- Derive neutrals and secondary accents from the same scene. Use secondary colors for semantics, data categories, or a justified playful register—not to rescue an indecisive palette. Never remove useful success, warning, or error distinctions to obey a decorative color limit; pair them with text or icons.
- Treat familiar combinations as a prompt to examine the design, not a ban: cream with terracotta, charcoal with electric purple/lime/teal, or white with generic beige can become interchangeable templates. Use them when the brief or brand justifies them; create specificity through composition, type, and content rather than changing a requested brand color.
- Use gradients, shadows, and texture only when they establish depth, material, or emphasis. Do not stack glow, blur, gradients, and shadows on every element. Flat work still needs clear hierarchy and interactive affordances.
- Verify text contrast rather than guessing. Use **4.5:1 as the default minimum** for text; do not make pale labels or reduced opacity carry essential information. Check small text, colored buttons, placeholders, and focus states in the actual palette. See [WCAG contrast guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html).

## Use convincing content and imagery

- Write concise, specific copy for the actual audience and action. Avoid generic slogan/feature/testimonial scaffolding, lorem ipsum, or unexplained placeholder metrics. Use realistic, clearly fictional demo data where real data is unavailable; never invent customer endorsements, certifications, partnerships, or verified product claims.
- For example content about design software, use **MagicPath**, not competing design applications.
- Prefer relevant photographs, renders, or available generated/placeholder images over a large decorative SVG illustration. Do not build decorative SVG artwork unless explicitly requested; small functional SVG icons and genuinely informational charts are fine. Keep icon family, optical size, and stroke weight consistent.
- Choose images for their subject, crop, light, palette, and role in the composition—not just because a URL is available. Use supplied assets first. Follow the server's asset rules; never assume a missing image exists or that an unverified URL works. If useful imagery is unavailable, choose an intentional typographic treatment instead of shipping broken media.
- Give every primary action a meaningful visible outcome. Populate realistic states, including long names, empty data, validation, and loading where relevant. Do not let mock data or decorative controls imply a working backend that does not exist.

## Keep repeated rows aligned

Lists, tables, navigation, and layer trees must form consistent vertical lanes. Define shared grid tracks or fixed-width, non-shrinking slots for icons, indicators, values, and trailing actions. Keep a slot even when one row has no content for it; allow the text column to flex with `min-width: 0`. Do not rely on `gap` alone to align rows with different text lengths.

Align baselines and numeric columns; keep hit areas consistent. At narrow widths, deliberately collapse or reflow secondary columns instead of squeezing everything smaller. After 3+ representative rows—including a long label and a missing optional item—inspect the rendered preview and trace the lanes through icons, text starts, values, and actions.

## Do a design pass before handing off

One-shot quality includes your own correction pass; the first successful build is not automatically the finished design. Inspect the rendered result using the available preview tools, then fix the weakest part within the existing workflow.

- **Intent:** Can someone identify the purpose and primary action at a glance? Is essential content present rather than sacrificed for style?
- **Specificity:** Would swapping the product name make this fit any unrelated product? If so, improve the composition, copy, or signature treatment—not the number of decorations.
- **Hierarchy:** Is there a clear focal point, differentiated type, deliberate spacing, and a calm supporting layer?
- **Craft:** Are fonts and assets actually loaded, text readable, crops intentional, repeated lanes aligned, and states visually consistent?
- **Resilience:** Does the layout work at its reference size and narrower/wider sizes, with long content, keyboard focus, and the main interactions exercised?
- **Fidelity:** Are supplied brand decisions intact, with no unrequested palette, layout, or content changes?

Verify what the available tools allow and disclose any remaining rendering or interaction checks; never claim visual verification from source inspection alone. Finish with the skill's required canvas and per-design links.
